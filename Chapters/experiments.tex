\section{Arm Calibration}
This section details the calibration process undertaken to validate the robotic
arm's positional accuracy and the correctness of the inverse kinematics solver.
The primary objective is to verify that the robotic arm functions as intended,
specifically checking whether the arm can accurately traverse to a set of desired Cartesian poses (position and orientation) within its workspace. This validation is essential to bridge the gap between the theoretical kinematic model and the physical reality of the constructed manipulator. Discrepancies often arise due to factors such as manufacturing tolerances in 3D-printed components, assembly variances, and potential backlash in gear reductions. Consequently, the theoretical mapping from joint angles to Cartesian coordinates must be validated to ensure reliable operation and precise motion control.

The calibration methodology involves a systematic "pose-check" routine. First, a set of target Cartesian 
coordinates $(x, y, z)$ distributed throughout the manipulator's reachable workspace are defined in the control software. 
Second, the Inverse Kinematics (IK) solver processes these targets to compute the required joint 
angles ($\theta_1, \theta_2, \dots$) necessary to achieve the desired end-effector position. Third, these angular 
values are converted into discrete motor steps and transmitted to the actuators to drive the physical arm. Finally, 
the actual resulting pose of the end-effector is visually and physically inspected against the calculated projection. 
This allows for the verification of link lengths and joint offsets in the software model, ensuring the physical motion 
aligns with the calculated trajectory. The implementation of the inverse kinematics solver and the associated calibration 
routines are available in the project's source code repository \cite{robotarmrepo}. As shown in the following images, all 
pose and accuracy checks were successfully passed. The visual comparison confirms that the physical arm accurately 
replicates the calculated poses derived from the kinematic model.

\section{End-to-End Target Acquisition}
This experiment evaluates the integrated system's ability to autonomously detect, localize, and 
approach a specific target in a simulated harvesting scenario. The process combines the computer 
vision pipeline described in Chapter 6 with the validated kinematic control system.

The procedure commences with the synchronous acquisition of raw RGB imagery and depth data, 
which is  structured into a 3D point cloud in ply formate. To facilitate target resolution, this 
volumetric data is projected onto a 2D image plane as shown on chapter 6.4 . Following this, the trained neural network 
performs instance segmentation using out trained model on the RGB frame as outlined on chapter 6.2,
yielding a binary mask that delineates the 
target morphology. This mask is then superimposed onto the 2D projection, enabling the isolation 
of relevant pixels. By leveraging the established spatial correspondence between the 2D 
projection and the original 3D data, the system effectively back-projects the segmented 
region to retrieve the specific 3D coordinates associated with the target object. Crucially, this 2D mask is then used to filter the original 3D dataset. By projecting the mask 
boundaries back into the 3D space, the system segments the specific subset of points belonging 
to the target object, effectively isolating it from the background and surrounding clutter.

\section{Harvesting Cycle Execution}
Following the successful acquisition of target coordinates, the system executes a multi-stage 
physical harvesting routine. This cycle is designed to ensure safe interaction with the crop 
and reliable detachment.

The operation sequence commences with an approach phase where the robot arm moves to 
a "pre-grasp" waypoint, defined with a 15 cm safety offset from the calculated target location. 
This offset prevents accidental collisions during the primary transit move. Subsequently, the 
arm executes a linear approach, closing the 15 cm distance at a controlled speed to reach the 
final interaction point. Once positioned, the gripper is closed to secure the target spear, 
followed by the activation of the cutting mechanism to sever the stalk. Upon completion of the 
cut, the manipulator retracts to a neutral "stand-up" home pose, effectively extracting the 
harvested produce from the workspace and resetting the system for the next operation.

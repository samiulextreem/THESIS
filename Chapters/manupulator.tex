\chapter{MANIPULATOR DESIGN \& PROTOTYPING}

This chapter presents the comprehensive design, development, and prototyping methodology for a robotic manipulator system. The development 
process encompasses computer-aided design (CAD) modeling, systematic material selection, component sourcing, assembly procedures, and comprehensive 
testing protocols.

\section{Requirements and Performance Specifications}

The manipulator design is governed by functional and performance requirements derived from the target application. The system must provide 
sufficient reach and workspace to access targets without frequent base repositioning, offer adequate degrees of freedom for precise positioning
 in constrained environments, and support standardized interfaces for interchangeable end-effectors.

In addition to these primary objectives, the design process is guided by constraints related to cost, manufacturability, maintainability,
 and operational safety. Requirements are systematically elicited from the target use-case and translated into verifiable performance metrics that can be 
 validated experimentally. Particular emphasis is placed on robustness to environmental disturbances, repeatability under repeated actuation 
 cycles, and energy efficiency within the specified power budget. The specification envelope balances competing objectives
  (payload versus inertia, stiffness versus weight, accuracy versus cost) using a conservative design margin to ensure reliability in field 
  conditions.


\section{Manipulator Architecture and Components}

The manipulator employs a 2-degree-of-freedom (2-DOF) configuration with a base joint (360° range) and a shoulder joint (360° range).
Link design utilizes T-slot 4040 aluminum for the base and shoulder links to achieve an optimal strength-to-weight ratio, reduced inertia, and 
lower cost, while employing
3D-printed PLA for joint housings to facilitate rapid prototyping. Actuator
selection includes NEMA 17 stepper motors with a 30:1 gear reduction for the base joint and a NEMA 17 stepper motor for the shoulder joint.
The overall kinematic structure is selected to provide a well-conditioned workspace for the intended task while limiting dynamic coupling
and minimizing reflected inertia at the joints. Material choices follow a mass-stiffness optimization rationale: aluminum members provide
structural rigidity at critical load-bearing links, whereas lightweight 3D-printed PLA housings reduce moving mass, improve acceleration 
capabilities, and
mitigate overshoot. Transmission ratios are selected to balance motor torque amplification against backdrivability and positioning
resolution, with microstepping control enhancing smoothness at low speeds. The architecture is modular, enabling substitution
of end-effectors and straightforward integration of alternative sensors (e.g., force/torque or proximity) without modifications 
to the primary structure.

\subsection*{Kinematic parameters and workspace}
For analysis and control, the arm is modeled as a planar 2-DOF serial chain with revolute joints. The link lengths are
\(L_1 = 0.25\,\mathrm{m}\) and \(L_2 = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}\). The nominal planar reach measured from the base frame to the tool tip spans the annulus
\([\,|L_1-L_2|,\; L_1+L_2\,] = [\,0.05\,\mathrm{m},\; 0.45\,\mathrm{m}\,]\), neglecting self-collisions and end-effector offsets.

\section{Electrical System Design}

The system architecture incorporates a 12\,V DC main power supply (5\,A capacity). The control architecture features two Arduino Nano microcontrollers,
A4988 stepper motor drivers with microstepping capability (1.8° per step), USB and wireless communication interfaces, and comprehensive 
safety systems including emergency stop, limit switches, and current monitoring. Wiring employs shielded cables for signal integrity,
strain relief and cable guides for mechanical protection, color-coded wiring for identification, and modular connectors for easy disassembly.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{pictures/arm_schamatic.png}
\caption{Electrical schematic of the robot arm control system, showing controller, drivers, and power distribution.}
\label{fig:arm_schematic}
\end{figure}

Two Arduino Nano microcontrollers implement a distributed control architecture. An on-board voltage regulator regulates the supply to 
5\,V for the Arduino Nano. Each board receives joint-level position commands for its
assigned link via an independent serial interface and performs local trajectory generation, thereby decoupling the control loops for the base 
and shoulder. For the base joint, the software accounts for an effective transmission ratio of 30:1 when converting commanded angles to motor 
steps and velocity profiles.

Both actuators are driven by closed-loop stepper motor drivers with microstepping to enhance positioning accuracy and disturbance rejection. The controllers 
interface with the microcontrollers using standard ENABLE, STEP, and DIRECTION control signals (as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:arm_schematic}). The 
drivers commutate the motor phases by switching the A$+$, A$-$, B$+$, and B$-$ outputs; the polarity is governed by the DIRECTION input while 
incremental motion is effected by STEP pulses. Microstepping is employed to achieve smoother motion and increased effective resolution. The closed-loop 
control implementation details are provided in Appendix~A.


\section{Power Consumption Analysis}

Accurate estimation of power consumption is critical for agricultural field deployment, where the robot must operate autonomously for extended 
periods between recharging cycles. To characterize the electrical power requirements of the manipulator, empirical measurements were acquired 
using a current shunt and voltage sensor connected to each motor driver output, with data sampled at 10\,Hz during typical motion sequences. 
The system operates at a supply voltage of 15\,V DC.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{pictures/pwr_motr1.png}
\caption{Measured voltage, current, and power for Motor 1 (base joint with 30:1 gear reduction) during typical positioning motion. 
Supply voltage is stable at 15\,V; current oscillates between 0.02\,A and 0.18\,A; instantaneous power varies from 0.3\,W to 2.7\,W.}
\label{fig:pwr_motr1}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{pictures/pwr_motr2.png}
\caption{Measured voltage, current, and power for Motor 2 (shoulder joint, direct drive) during typical positioning motion. 
Supply voltage is stable at 15\,V; current oscillates between 0.01\,A and 0.20\,A; instantaneous power varies from 0.2\,W to 3.0\,W.}
\label{fig:pwr_motr2}
\end{figure}

Figures~\ref{fig:pwr_motr1} and~\ref{fig:pwr_motr2} present the measured voltage, current, and instantaneous power for Motor~1 
(base joint with 30:1 gear reduction) and Motor~2 (shoulder joint, direct drive) during representative positioning operations. 
The experimental data reveal that actual power consumption is significantly lower than the motors' rated capacity, owing to the 
microstepping driver's current modulation and the relatively light load imposed by the manipulator's kinematic design.

The supply voltage remains stable at 15\,V DC across all operating conditions (variance $<$0.02\,V). Motor~1 exhibits a peak current 
of 0.18\,A, yielding peak power $P_1^{\mathrm{peak}} \approx 2.7$\,W; the 30:1 gear reduction reduces the torque demand on the motor, 
resulting in lower current draw compared to direct-drive configurations. Motor~2 shows a peak current of 0.20\,A, yielding peak power 
$P_2^{\mathrm{peak}} \approx 3.0$\,W; the direct-drive configuration exhibits slightly higher current during motion but remains well 
below rated capacity. Both current and power waveforms exhibit characteristic microstepping oscillations as the driver modulates phase 
currents to produce smooth incremental motion. The oscillation frequency corresponds to the step rate commanded during motion segments.

Time-averaged power during continuous motion (computed over the recorded samples) is approximately 1.2--1.5\,W for Motor~1 and 
1.3--1.6\,W for Motor~2. During holding (motor energized but stationary), current drops to approximately 0.03--0.05\,A, 
corresponding to a holding power of 0.5--0.8\,W per motor. Auxiliary electronics (Arduino Nano microcontrollers, voltage regulators, 
communication modules) collectively consume an estimated 1.5--2.0\,W under nominal conditions. Based on these measurements, the 
manipulator exhibits remarkably low power requirements during typical operation, with peak transient power consumption below 10\,W 
and time-averaged power below 5\,W.

For extended field deployment, a conservative energy budget estimate assumes an 8-hour work shift with the robot operating 
continuously in idle or active harvesting mode. Using the measured time-averaged power of approximately 4--5\,W for typical operation, 
the total energy requirement is approximately $E_{\mathrm{total}} \approx 5\,\mathrm{W} \times 8\,\mathrm{h} = 40$\,W$\cdot$h. To account 
for power supply conversion inefficiencies (85--90\% for DC-DC converters and stepper drivers), environmental effects (temperature-dependent 
battery capacity degradation, voltage sag under load), and battery aging (capacity fade over charge cycles), a recommended battery capacity 
of 50--60\,W$\cdot$h is specified. A 15\,V, 3.5\,A\,h lithium-ion battery pack (nominal capacity 52.5\,W\,h) or a 12\,V, 5\,A\,h 
lithium-polymer pack (60\,W\,h) satisfies this requirement while maintaining compatibility with the measured 15\,V supply voltage and 
providing adequate margin for adverse conditions.

\section{Fabrication and Assembly}
Manufacturing methods include CNC machining for aluminum components (precision and durability), 3D printing for PLA plastic housings and brackets, 
manual assembly for component integration and cable routing, and calibration procedures for joint alignment and sensor setup. Dimensional 
tolerances are specified for parts alignment and shaft interfaces to ensure minimal backlash and consistent transmission 
efficiency. Quality control inspections are performed after each major operation, including visual inspection, dimensional verification with 
calipers/gauges, and rotational smoothness checks of assembled joints. All project CAD files referenced in this chapter are provided in 
Appendix~A for completeness and reproducibility.

The assembly sequence follows: (1) Base assembly with motor mounting and wiring, (2) Shoulder joint integration with gear reduction, (3) Forearm 
assembly with cable routing, (4) Wrist mechanism and end-effector mounting, (5) Electrical system integration and testing, (6) Final calibration 
and performance validation. Post-assembly calibration includes homing routine verification, and zero-offset determination 
for each joint to establish a consistent reference frame for control and testing.

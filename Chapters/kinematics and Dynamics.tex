\section{Design Choices}

The arm employs a 30:1 servo gear reduction, selected from preliminary analysis to deliver the 
required joint torques while conserving power during extended field operation. The mechanism comprises 
three revolute joints (R1, R2, R3). We analyze worst-case static torques in a horizontal configuration (maximum gravity moment arms) 
using \(L_1 = 0.25\,\mathrm{m}\) and \(L_2 = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}\).

Joint torque requirements were derived under a fully extended worst-case configuration. Gravitational 
loading at the shoulder and elbow is approximated as $F_2 = 0.5$\,kg $\times$ $9.8$\,m/s$^2$ $= 4.5$\,N and $F_3 = 0.3$\,kg $\times$ $9.8$\,m/s$^2$ $= 2.98$\,N, where the $0.5$\,kg at R2 aggregates the stepper motor and ancillary components (electronics, PLA housing, fasteners). These forces inform motor sizing and gear selection. The mass distribution prioritizes mobility: the total mass at R2 is approximately $0.5$\,kg (a $0.3$\,kg NEMA~17 plus about $0.2$\,kg ancillary components), R3 is about $0.3$\,kg, and the fingertip is limited to $\leq 15$\,g to minimize distal inertia. Links are specified in lightweight materials (aluminum or wood) to balance durability and energy efficiency; heavier links would impose unnecessary current draw and reduce battery life in uneven asparagus fields.
Concretely, the $0.5$\,kg at R2 is composed of $0.3$\,kg for the motor and approximately $0.2$\,kg for electronics, PLA housing, and fasteners. At 
R1 (the base), grounded structure mass is not included in torque calculations because it is not accelerated by any upstream joint.
Define gravitational loads: $F_{\mathrm{R2}}=0.5\,\mathrm{kg}\cdot 9.8=4.90\,\mathrm{N}$, $F_{\mathrm{R3}}=0.3\,\mathrm{kg}\cdot 9.8=2.94\,\mathrm{N}$, and $F_{\mathrm{tip}}=0.015\,\mathrm{kg}\cdot 9.8=0.147\,\mathrm{N}$. The worst-case elbow torque (R2) for horizontal links is
\[
T_2=(F_{\mathrm{R3}}+F_{\mathrm{tip}})\,L_2\; +\; m_{L_2}\,g\,\tfrac{L_2}{2} \quad (\text{optional link mass term}).
\]
Neglecting link mass, $T_2\approx(2.94+0.147)\,\mathrm{N}\cdot 0.20\,\mathrm{m}=0.617\,\mathrm{N\,m}$. The base torque (R1) accumulates distal loads at their lever arms:
\[
T_1=F_{\mathrm{R2}}\,L_1+(F_{\mathrm{R3}}+F_{\mathrm{tip}})(L_1+L_2)\; +\; m_{L_1}\,g\,\tfrac{L_1}{2}+m_{L_2}\,g\,(L_1+\tfrac{L_2}{2}).
\]
Neglecting link masses, $T_1\approx 4.90\,\mathrm{N}\cdot 0.25\,\mathrm{m}+3.087\,\mathrm{N}\cdot 0.45\,\mathrm{m}=2.61\,\mathrm{N\,m}$. Applying 
a 1.3 safety factor for dynamics and payload variability 
gives $T_2^{\mathrm{req}}\approx0.80\,\mathrm{N\,m}$ and $T_1^{\mathrm{req}}\approx3.39\,\mathrm{N\,m}$. These expressions allow direct 
inclusion of link masses if later specified.

Each joint is actuated by a NEMA~17 stepper selected for compactness and robustness, providing a 
rated torque of $0.4$\,N\,m. With the 30:1 transmission, the available output torque is 
approximately $12$\,N\,m. Relative to the requirements above (neglecting link masses), this yields 
$m_{\mathrm{margin},R2} \approx 12/0.80 \approx 15\times$ at R2 and $m_{\mathrm{margin},R1} \approx 12/3.39 \approx 3.5\times$ at R1, providing ample headroom for uncertainties and transient dynamics. 








\section{CAD Model and Simulation}


The robotic arm model, crafted in Autodesk Inventor, serves as a foundational 
design for autonomous asparagus harvesting, emphasizing precision and 
efficiency in agricultural field conditions. The model showcases a three-joint 
configuration, with a base joint (R1) featuring a robust motor assembly, 
depicted as a red and black circular component, providing the necessary torque 
for stable root revolution. The first link extends from the base to the elbow 
joint (R2), measuring approximately 0.25 m, and is supported by a blue 
structural frame to handle the mechanical stresses during operation. 

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{inventor/inventor.png}
    \caption{Robot arm design in Inventor}
    \label{fig:cad_inventor}
\end{figure}




The second 
link, spanning 0.2 m from the elbow to the end effector (R3), leads to a finger 
tip mechanism designed for delicate grasping of asparagus spears. The arm’s 
construction prioritizes lightweight materials, such as aluminum or wood, 
ensuring the total weight at the elbow joint is around 0.46 kg and at the end 
effector approximately 0.3 kg, with the finger tip weighing a mere 15 g to 
minimize energy demands in the field. 






The design’s sleek and elongated structure reduces soil disturbance in 
asparagus fields, while its three degrees of freedom (base, shoulder, elbow)
enable precise maneuvering to reach low-lying spears. 
Integrated with an AI-driven computer vision system, the arm employs advanced 
detection and segmentation to identify mature asparagus spears, guiding the 
finger tip to execute soft-grip actions that prevent damage to the plant’s 
crown. This model represents a practical step toward automating asparagus 
harvesting, reducing labor dependency, and enhancing sustainability by ensuring 
high-quality yields with minimal environmental impact.

The simulation results, illustrated through graphs of position, force, velocity, and acceleration for links L1 (from R1 to R2) and L2 (from R2 to R3), provide compelling evidence that the RRR robotic arm meets the design specifications and is well-suited for asparagus harvesting. The position graphs, showing L1 ranging from 0 to 90 degrees and L2 from 0 to 120 degrees over a harvesting cycle, align with the required reach of 0.45 m (0.25 m for L1 and 0.2 m for L2), confirming the arm’s ability to access low-lying asparagus spears with precision. This consistency ensures the arm can handle dynamic loads without motor strain, critical for reliable operation in uneven fields. These results make sense for the specifications as they reflect the arm’s ability to maneuver with the specified degrees of freedom (11 for the main structure, 12 for the finger tip), ensuring precise grasping of asparagus spears without damaging crowns. The data also supports field performance under varying conditions (e.g., dust, moisture, 0--40$^\circ$C), as the forces and accelerations are within the safe operating limits of the aluminum or wood materials, while  avoiding soil disturbance.
